A deck swap and two data bugs: 600 → 1023 on the Pokémon ladder

Three months on Kaggle's Pokémon TCG AI Battle ladder (June to August 2026): the lab's side campaign in decision-making under imperfect information. The agent that held is a plain MLP behavior-cloned on a million corrected replay decisions, flying the meta's best decks. The three largest gains of the whole effort were a deck swap and two data-pipeline bug fixes, and nearly every modeling lever gated null with a mechanism attached. This is the summary: the solution, the arc, what paid, what didn't, and what transfers.

1. the problem, and why it counts here

The Pokémon TCG is an imperfect-information game: the opponent's hand is hidden, both players' deck order and prizes are hidden, and coin flips decide real outcomes. The engine calls your agent at every decision point with the board state and a list of legal options; you return indices. The ladder updates a gaussian skill rating on wins and losses only, never game score, and every submission enters at μ=600. Everything you can run locally (a Docker sim, seat-alternated A/Bs, a gauntlet of field decks mined from replays) is a proxy. The ladder is the only instrument that measures the thing being ranked.

For this lab the appeal was exactly that: the same discipline the milestones run under (controls, pre-registered gates, honest numbers) pointed at an adversarial scoreboard that cannot be argued with. The campaign ran off the milestone path, but it stress-tested the lab's methodology harder than any milestone has.

2. the arc, in six phases

June · heuristics and search. A greedy heuristic (evolve, attach, develop the board, highest-damage attack) beat random ~92%. On top of it, determinized search: sample the hidden zones, roll each world forward through the engine's own forward model, average. Cross-turn lookahead (horizon ≥ 2) was the whole edge: within one turn, greedy was already near-optimal. Live: ~602 on an off-meta combo deck.

June · the deck lesson. The same agent on the field's #1 deck moved 540 → ~912. The largest single gain of the campaign, and one self-play is structurally blind to: mirrors cancel the deck.

June to July · learned components hit their ceilings. PUCT with a self-play-trained value flopped live at 538, recovered to ~820 with a value grounded in field replays, and plateaued at the heuristic's level. The value ceiling (~0.75 val-acc) held across five representations: informational, not architectural. RL on top of the imitation policy: seven configurations, all flat.

July · meta-chasing and the harvest. Archetype pilots (Alakazam 819, Garchomp ~803, Archaludon, Grimmsnarl), a C++ rollout search, and a 22.5k-episode harvest of top-band ladder replays. Live archetype pilots kept beating every local proxy's prediction.

July 28 · the alignment bug. Replay actions answer the previous step's observation: every imitation label was shifted by one. The fix took the corrected pilot's game gate from 13–34% to 95.4% and spawned the il2 family, whose arch pair peaked at 904.6.

August · frozen weights and the second bug. A dozen refinement levers gated null and the weights froze. Deck censuses steered submissions through the Dragapult wave. Then the second structural bug, found in the final week: fetch decisions were featurized card-blind (a zeroed source-card id). The fix was worth ~+19 points on four independent gates, and the fixed build (drgfx) hit 1022.9 live. The field's #1 finished on a structural counter-deck: a basics-toolbox Kangaskhan going 15–4 into Dragapult.

3. the solution that held

Strip the story to its ending and the shipped agent is almost embarrassingly plain: no search at play time, no RL, no transformer. A feed-forward network, behavior-cloned on roughly a million decisions mined from top-band ladder replays, playing the meta's best decks. Everything that made it strong happened in the data: the harvest was large, the labels correctly paired, the features actually populated.

Those last two clauses carried the campaign, because both were false for most of it. The label-alignment bug (actions off by one observation) silently poisoned every offline imitation metric for a month. The fetch-featurization bug made the whole model family card-blind on the most common decision type until the final week. Both hid the same way: the model was trained and validated on the same wrong data, so offline top-1 improved (0.51 → 0.57) while the live game gate collapsed (34% → 13%). A better fit to the wrong target is a worse policy. Only numbers measured by playing games survived the audit.

4. what paid, ranked

1 · deck-vs-field fit. +~370 live from one swap. Deck strength against the field is invisible to every local proxy: only the ladder measures it.
2 · fixing the label-alignment bug. One index shift in replay extraction. The fix was worth more than all architecture work combined.
3 · fixing the fetch-blind featurization. One zeroed feature on the most common decision type. ~+19 points across four independent gates.
4 · high-volume corrected behavior cloning. A plain MLP on ~1M correctly-paired decisions beat every hand-built agent. Volume beat curation: leader-only subsets added nothing.
5 · cross-turn search on an honest forward model. +~10 points over greedy in the heuristic era, fading as pilots got stronger.
6 · live replay mining as diagnosis. Never directly rating-positive, but it produced every precise finding: the anti-aggro hole, the Ogerpon weakness mechanism, the deck censuses that steered the final submissions.

5. what didn't: closed, with mechanism

Each of these was closed by a fair, seat-alternated game gate, and each closure came with a reason. That is what makes the list safe to trust.

Better value functions. Capped at ~0.75 val-acc across five representations (scalar MLP, attention, sequence GRU, card-embedding transformer, heuristic). The limit is informational: the game is near-Markovian and the residual is unseen future draws. No architecture breaks it.
Distilling search into the policy. Flat even re-run correctly. 46% of searched decisions have flat Q-values: the policy learns the value's shape, not a better ranking. The search edge is play-time only.
RL past the imitation cap. REINFORCE, PPO (with and without critic warm-up and reward shaping), SIL, self-play curriculum: seven configurations, all flat. The opponent was too weak to carry signal, and small-scale self-play degraded rather than compounded.
Deeper or wider search. The agent used 3–7% of its 420 s time bank, yet paired 32-vs-256-world tests showed extra depth makes candidate moves converge. Never size a search knob from an unpaired sweep.
Specialist fine-tunes and deck cloning. Three separate failures with one shape: pass the mirror, fail the field. Specialists overfit the self-play opponent, and cloned decks lose without their original pilots.
More heuristic guards. One guard fixed one of five losses in its target matchup. The chronic holes were damage math and matchup structure, not piloting.
Rules exploits. Stall-to-draw, timeout attrition, mill, deck-stacking: draws were 6 of 14,821 games, timeouts 5. The engine and ladder leave nothing there.

6. twelve laws, ordered by what they cost to learn

1the ground truth is the game, not the metric: offline top-1 rose .51 → .57 while live play fell 34% → 13% 2audit labels and features before touching architecture: two data bugs out-earned months of modeling 3know what each proxy cannot see: self-play cancels the deck · saturated gauntlets cannot rank the top 4measure the ceiling before you scale: one maximally-advantaged iteration killed a multi-day run, correctly 5engineer the variance before trusting any delta: byte-identical submissions diverged σ≈48 live 6distribution shift is the default read on better-offline-worse-live (agent vs expert states: auc .83) 7score the decision, not the index: a policy at .34 agreement beat one at .49 · the game gate decides 8record negatives with mechanism, not mood: 40+ closed levers stayed navigable, re-litigation stayed cheap 9search pays only where the value is honest: a bc value head lost rating to search, an on-policy head gained 10strategy beats sophistication: the winner ran a counter-deck · a full bc → ppo → mcts pipeline placed 552nd 11hard operational gates prevent unforced errors: zero validation failures across dozens of submissions 12volume beats curation for imitation: everything correctly labeled beat every curated subset

7. what transfers

The toolkit that survived every methodological audit: a process-isolated, seat-alternated A/B harness. A field gauntlet of real opponent decks mined from replays, the first offline proxy for strength-against-the-field. A divergence check that asks whether a change alters any decision at all before gating it. The make-or-break test: one maximally-advantaged iteration before any long training commitment. And the research log plus memory index, negatives recorded with mechanism, which is the reason three months of results stayed composable instead of becoming folklore.

Unfired ammunition, if the arena reopens: an on-policy value head for search (the field's post-mortems say a behavior-cloned head loses 20–30 rating points to test-time search and an on-policy head gains 50–70). A DAgger-style on-policy correction for the distribution shift we diagnosed and never treated. And a native-speed harness from day one: the engine compiled natively ~50× faster than under emulation, discovered months in, after every sweep had been noise-limited by throughput.

None of this is Pokémon-specific. Law 1 is the lesson milestone 13 taught at home (loss and downstream capability coming apart), and law 5 is the lab's standing eval-resolution blocker wearing tournament clothes. The rating was the score. The map of what pays, what doesn't, and how to tell the difference cheaply is the deliverable.
